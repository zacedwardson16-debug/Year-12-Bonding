Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice. A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. In a co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond, both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom, and we draw it as an arrow.

Is a co-ordinate bond different once it forms?

No. In NH₄⁺ all four N–H bonds are identical: same length, same strength. The arrow only records where the two electrons came from. The donor needs a lone pair; the acceptor needs room for two more electrons (H⁺ has none; B in BF₃ and Al in AlCl₃ have only six outer electrons).

There are four types of crystal structure: ionic, metallic, macromolecular (giant covalent) and molecular. Melting a substance means overcoming the forces that hold its particles in place, so the type of force decides the melting point. Metallic bonding is the attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.

Bonding pairs and lone pairs are charge clouds that repel each other, so they spread out as far apart as possible. Lone pairs repel more strongly: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair. Each lone pair squeezes the bond angles a little (about 2.5° in a tetrahedral arrangement).     Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond. When the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the electrons are shared unequally and the bond is polar: we show this with δ+ and δ−. A molecule with polar bonds has a permanent dipole only if its shape does not make the bond dipoles cancel.

    Three forces act between molecules: van der Waals forces (induced dipole–dipole, also called dispersion or London forces) act between all molecules; permanent dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules; hydrogen bonds form when H is bonded to N, O or F and is attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule. Boiling a molecular substance overcomes these forces: the covalent bonds do not break.
